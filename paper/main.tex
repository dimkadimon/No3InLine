\title{A super-polylogarithmic excess in the\texorpdfstring{\\}{ }no-three-in-line problem}
\author{Dmitry Kamenetsky}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Let $F_{\mathrm{ht}}(n)$ be the largest size of a half-turn invariant subset of the $n\times n$ integer grid with no three points collinear. For infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod{840}$ we prove that
\[
 \mathcopy{F_ht(4p + 1) >= 6p + 2 + exp(log p / (300 log log p)).}{F_{\mathrm{ht}}(4p+1)\ \ge\ 6p+2+
 \exp\!\left(\frac{\log p}{300\log\log p}\right).}
\]
Thus the guaranteed excess over $3n/2$ on these sides grows faster than every fixed power of $\log n$. We start with a four-period modular hyperbola ring and eight boundary points. A common conic level admits many pairs of opposite interior points whose obstructing secants use distinct multiplicative cosets. Hall's theorem then selects orientations of the ring that admit all these pairs without decreasing its size. Stewart's theorem on prime factors of cyclotomic values supplies the required subgroup indices. The result concerns a second-order term on a subsequence; it does not raise the leading coefficient $3/2$. We also give finite certificates and compare their sizes with explicit external constructions.
\end{abstract}
\section{The excess above the classical bound}\label{sec:intro}
Write $[n]_0=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$. A \emph{cap} is a set of lattice points in which no three distinct points are collinear on an ordinary Euclidean line. Let $F(n)$ be the largest size of a cap in $[n]_0^2$, and let $\htf(n)$ be the corresponding maximum under the additional requirement of invariance under $(x,y)\mapsto(n-1-x,n-1-y)$. In particular, $\htf(n)\le F(n)\le 2n$: each row holds at most two points. Modular lines enter the construction but do not replace ordinary lines in either definition.

Hall, Jackson, Sudbery and Wild~\cite[Theorem 2]{hjsw} constructed $3(q-1)$ points on side $2q$ for prime $q$, that is, $3n/2-3$ at their native side. Embedding at a nearby smaller prime gives $F(n)\ge (3/2-o(1))n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. The modern treatment of Kov\'acs, Nagy and Szab\'o~\cite[Theorem 1.2]{kns} retains this bound for the case in which at most two points may be collinear. A different question is whether the \emph{excess} $F(n)-3n/2$ can be guaranteed to grow along a sequence. This comparison is with $3n/2$ at the actual side $n$, not with the count after rounding down to a convenient prime.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod{840}$, with $n=4p+1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 \htf(n)\ \ge\ \frac{3n}{2}+\frac12+g(p),
 \qquad g(p)=\exp\!\left(\frac{\log p}{300\log\log p}\right).
\end{equation}
The cap consists of six points per nonzero hyperbola fibre, eight fixed boundary points and additional interior points in opposite pairs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:superpoly}
For every fixed $A>0$, the half-turn excess on the sides in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} satisfies
\[
  \mathcopy{lim (n -> infinity) (F_ht(n) - 3n/2) / (log n)^A = +infinity.}{\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\htf(n)-\tfrac32 n}{(\log n)^A}=+\infty.}
\]
The same limit holds for the unrestricted excess $F(n)-\tfrac32 n$, with the same normalization and along the same sequence.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof} Since $n=4p+1$, $\log\log n=\log\log p+o(1)$, so
\[
  \log\!\left(\frac{g(p)}{(\log n)^A}\right)
  =\frac{\log p}{300\log\log p}-A\log\log n\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
Now apply~\eqref{eq:main} and $F(n)\ge\htf(n)$.
\end{proof}
The displayed \emph{guarantee} is also $o(n^\varepsilon)$ for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$. This is not an upper bound on the true excess. In particular the argument proves neither a fixed positive improvement of the coefficient $3/2$ nor any frequency estimate for the good sides.

\paragraph{Where the extra points come from.} A four-period conic ring already has $6(p-1)$ points; eight suitable boundary points bring its size to $6p+2=3n/2+1/2$. The obstacle is that two ring points can determine a secant through a proposed interior site. We keep all six sites per fibre in cardinality, but not necessarily the same six sites in position. Each secant yields a choice: disable one of two endpoint orientations. If the abscissa cosets of all endpoint occurrences are distinct, any one orientation variable occurs at most twice. This permits one matching to satisfy all the choices, at all the new sites at once. The proof estimates the probability of failure of precisely this coset condition.

\paragraph{Comparison with the closest constructions.} The six-site masks were observed by conic\_\allowbreak lift\_\allowbreak synthesist~\cite[\S5.4]{masks}. The four-period ring, its lossless component flips and the Boolean clauses for insertions are from~\cite{ring}. At a nonsquare level and $p\equiv1\pmod{12}$ that ring has $6(p-1)=3n/2-6$ points on side $n=4p$. The linear-size target sets of~\cite{pair} and~\cite[Theorem 3]{ring} keep their pair-lines off the carrier but do not exclude all secants through two old sites. Conversely, the rooted identities of~\cite[\S2]{root} lead there to partial totals of $145p/24-o(p)$ and, on a subsequence, $4721p/768-o(p)$ on side $4p$~\cite[Theorem 1 and Corollary 4]{root}. Those unions can have triples through at least two targets outside a fixed finite set of directions. Neither partial result gives an all-direction cap of the combined size. The eight boundary points in an earlier, unpublished note of this project~\cite{boundary} give $6p+2$, a fixed excess of just $1/2$ on the present boards.

Here we require a \emph{single} orientation of the full ring for a growing family of simultaneous opposite-pair insertions. The estimates supplied here are the equal-slope lift count, the unequal-slope coset-collision bound and its shared-level two-root counterpart. They are proved below, not imported from search results. We rederive the geometric construction and rooted identities so that the proof does not rely on an unpublished note, a forum result or executable code. Its named external mathematical inputs are Hall's marriage theorem, the quadratic character bound and Stewart's prime-factor theorem (and elementary quadratic reciprocity). This comparison identifies specific antecedents; it does not establish worldwide priority for second-order bounds.
